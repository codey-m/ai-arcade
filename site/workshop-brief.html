          How the machine reads a brief

          The machine knows three ways to read an order. It builds a toy only where every reading that fits your brief agrees, and shows both designs where they don’t. If no reading fits, the line stops.

          How to play

          Letters. Four children wrote to Santa. The toy machine reads only each order’s label: the child, the toy, its color, and bedtime or outdoors.

          Brief. Pin one instruction strip and up to two example toys, approved or rejected. Then run a test batch.

          Test. Where your brief leaves a choice open, the machine shows both designs. When all four toys match their letters, send them to the sleigh. The fewer examples, the tighter the brief.

          New letters. Each mailbag brings four children who want different things, so a brief that worked before may not work again.

          The idea behind the game

          A brief has to carry what the letters say

          The machine could make every toy from the first test: it knew a dragon from a robot and blue from red. What it lacked was what these children wanted, because it read only the order labels and your brief.

          A general instruction rules out whole readings at once. An example earns its place when the readings still in play would build that toy differently; an example they already agree on changes nothing. People who write instructions for AI systems work the same way: say the general intent plainly, and add examples where the instruction still leaves a choice open.

          The machine’s readings are three fixed plans, listed under How it reads a brief. It builds only where every plan that fits your brief agrees. There is no live AI model and no hidden score.
